# Shrink the photos, keep the grid

Yes, Grocery Khata can show its items as a Flipkart-style picture card grid, and most of the pieces already exist. The store page renders `ItemCard` tiles with an `imageUrl`, and the phone already uploads a 512 px WebP square. Three changes are needed. First, make the layout **2 columns on phones (about 164 CSS px per card at 360 px width)** and 4 to 6 on desktop. Second, add a **second, smaller rendition of each photo (320 to 360 px, about 15 to 20 KB)** for the grid, and keep the 512 px image for the detail view. Third, tighten the byte budget, because the current 150 KB ceiling is roughly 3 to 5 times what a white-background pack shot needs. Product images can come from the internet, but only from sources that grant a licence. **Open Food Facts photos are CC BY-SA and allow commercial use with attribution**, and GS1 India DataKart offers brand-supplied images to paying subscribers. Copying pack shots from Flipkart, Amazon, BigBasket or Google Images is a real legal risk: Delhi High Court rulings in 2023 and 2024 went against sellers who reused other people's product photos. Memory depends on decoded pixels, not file size. A 512 px image takes about 1 MB of RAM once decoded and a 320 px image about 0.4 MB, so the grid thumbnail plus list virtualisation is what keeps cheap Android phones from killing the tab. One caution applies throughout: no public, measured specs for Flipkart, Blinkit or Zepto cards were found, so every layout number below is a starting value to check against your own screenshots.

## Two 164 px columns beat three 104 px columns on a 360 px phone

The big Indian apps share a recognisable card, though none publish its measurements. Blinkit's card data carries selling price, MRP, a discount percentage that is left out when price equals MRP, and an optional offer label ([Apify Blinkit scraper](https://apify.com/solidcode/blinkit-scraper)). A Zepto design case study describes tiles with thumbnails, discount badges and large "+" buttons on an 8 pt spacing grid ([Aadhavan M](https://aadhavanm.pages.dev/skills/visual-design)). The order is consistent: square image first, then name, pack size, price with struck MRP and green "% off", and finally an ADD button that turns into a −/qty/+ stepper. Flipkart's grid usually has no add button and swaps in rating chips. For a kirana store, Blinkit's pattern fits better, because pack size and price matter more than ratings. Baymard's research supports this: product lists need enough information to spare shoppers needless trips to product pages, and those trips cost more on mobile ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/product-list)).

The column count follows from arithmetic. At 360 px width, with 12 px side gutters and an 8 px gap, two columns give **(360 − 24 − 8) / 2 = 164 px** per card. Three columns give about 104 px, too narrow for a two-line name, a price row and an ADD button at readable sizes. This **overrides the 3-column suggestion in our earlier catalogue research**. Three columns still suit compact category tiles that carry no long text. On a phone, users see only about 4 to 6 grid items at a time, and in grids the image should take most of the space while text is shortened to avoid wrapping ([UX Movement](https://uxmovement.com/?p=8647)). "A little smaller on mobile" therefore means tighter padding and smaller type, not more columns.

A concrete Tailwind starting spec uses the grid `grid grid-cols-2 gap-2 sm:grid-cols-3 md:grid-cols-4 lg:grid-cols-5 xl:grid-cols-6`. Each card is `rounded-lg border p-2 md:p-3`. The image box is `aspect-square w-full object-contain bg-gray-50`. The name is `text-[13px] line-clamp-2` with a reserved two-line minimum height so rows line up, pack size is `text-xs text-gray-500`, price is `text-sm font-bold`, and MRP is `text-xs line-through`. For Hindi names, use `leading-snug` rather than `leading-tight` and set `lang="hi"` so matras are not clipped. The reliable clamp is the legacy `-webkit-line-clamp` form, which needs `overflow:hidden` to clip ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/line-clamp)). The ADD button can look 32 px tall, but its hit area should be padded to about **48 × 48 px with 8 px spacing** from other targets ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/accessible-tap-targets)). Use Tailwind green-700 (#15803d) or darker for "% off" text, because bright greens fail WCAG AA contrast on white; check the exact value with a contrast checker. Give the button an aria-label such as "Add Toor Dal 1 kg". Items without a photo keep the same square box and show today's emoji-on-gradient or a first-letter tile, and an `onError` handler swaps in that fallback, so the grid never collapses or shows a broken-image icon.

## Licensed sources only: shop photos first, Open Food Facts second, never scraped marketplaces

Under the Copyright Act 1957, a product photo is a copyrighted work owned by its photographer or brand. In a 2023 case against Meesho sellers, the Delhi High Court held that sellers have no right to copy a plaintiff's photographs, and it said platforms must verify seller details ([Verdictum](https://verdictum.in/court-updates/high-courts/sellers-do-not-have-right-to-copy-photographs-or-product-design-of-small-businesses-delhi-high-court-grants-injunction-against-meesho-for-copyright-infringement-1500150)). In Louis Vuitton v Haute24 (2024), the same court imposed **Rs 5 lakh costs for unauthorised use of product photographs** ([SCC Online](https://scconline.com/blog/post/2024/08/28/dhc-imposes-rs-5-lakhs-costs-on-website-for-using-copyrighted-photographs-of-louis-vuitton-without-authorization)). Marketplace terms also ban automated scraping; Amazon's Conditions of Use prohibit data-mining and extraction tools ([ConductAtlas](https://conductatlas.com/platform/amazon/amazon-conditions-of-use/provision/CA-P-018441/prohibition-on-data-mining-and-scraping-tools/)). Google Images only finds photos; it grants no licence to use them. A single kirana owner copying a pack shot is unlikely to be sued. A SaaS platform that does it systematically across thousands of shops is a far more visible target, and the Meesho reasoning about platform duties could reach us. We found no Indian case where a brand sued a reseller for using the brand's own pack shot to sell genuine goods, so an implied-licence argument may exist, but it needs an IP lawyer's opinion before anyone relies on it.

The legal internet sources are narrower. Open Food Facts licenses its images under **CC BY-SA 3.0 and allows commercial use**, provided we credit it with a link and share adapted images under the same licence. That licence covers only the photo, not the packaging design or trademarks ([OFF terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)). Its India coverage reached about **20,000 products by February 2026** ([India Food Stats](https://indiafoodstats.blogspot.com/2026/02/wikipedia-of-food-in-india-just-got.html)), against an estimated 90,000 food products sold in India ([OFF wiki](https://wiki.openfoodfacts.org/Dashboard/India)). It is mostly big FMCG brands, with nothing for loose or local goods. Open Products Facts uses the same licences for non-food items ([Open Products Facts](https://openproductsfacts.org/data)), but its coverage is thin. GS1 India DataKart holds brand-uploaded images, BigBasket has used it ([GS1 case study](https://admin.gs1india.org/uploads/Big_Basket_Case_Study_a809bd7489.pdf)), and API access is a paid Premium feature whose price we could not find ([DataKart Premium](https://www.gs1india.org/datakart/datakart-premium)). Cheap barcode APIs such as UPCitemdb (free for 100 requests a day; [ShopSavvy comparison](https://shopsavvy.com/compare/shopsavvy-data-api-vs-upcitemdb)) are useful for names, but their images are often scraped from retailers and carry no licence.

The cleanest source is the shop's own photo: the shopkeeper owns it, and the goods are genuine. Marketplaces ask for a white background, the product only, and no watermarks ([VTEX](https://newhelp.vtex.com/docs/tutorials/best-practices-for-using-images-in-the-catalog)). A square framing guide in the capture screen, plus automatic trimming of near-white borders and padding onto pure white, gets most of the way there at no cost. If shops agree to licence terms, a photo taken once can serve every shop that sells the same EAN. Medicines need extra care. The still-draft 2018 e-pharmacy rules bar e-pharmacies from advertising drugs on the internet ([Mondaq](https://mondaq.com/india/food-and-drugs-law/747792/centre-proposes-draft-e-pharmacy-rules-to-regulate-online-sale-of-drugs)), so the online store should show prescription items with a neutral icon.

## Memory is pixels times four, so the grid needs a 320 px rendition

File size and memory are different budgets. File bytes cost data and download time. Once the browser decodes an image, it holds about width × height × 4 bytes of RAM, no matter how small the file was. That is about **1.0 MB for 512², 0.52 MB for 360² and 0.41 MB for 320²**. This standard formula did not come from a fetched source this session, so check it against Chromium documentation if exact figures matter. A grid of 200 cards at 512 px that all stay decoded could hold around 200 MB, enough to get a tab killed on a 2 to 3 GB phone. At 320 px with virtualisation keeping about 30 cards alive, that falls to roughly 12 to 30 MB.

The pixel size a card needs is its CSS width times the device pixel ratio. A 164 to 180 px card needs about 340 px on a 2x phone and about 510 px on a 3x phone. A **320 to 360 px thumbnail plus the existing 512 px image** covers both cases if served with `srcset` and `sizes`, which let the browser pick the candidate that matches slot width × DPR ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/browser-level-image-lazy-loading)). Going above 512 px adds nothing at card size. On bytes, WebP quality 75 to 85 is the common range for product photos ([rewarx](https://www.rewarx.com/blogs/optimize-ecommerce-product-images-core-web-vitals-speed-2026)). Scaled to our pixel sizes, a white-background pack shot should land at roughly **15 to 30 KB at 512 px and 6 to 15 KB at 320 px**. These are estimates to measure on real catalogue photos. A 40-card page then costs about 600 KB instead of the up-to-6 MB that the current 150 KB ceiling allows, which matters on prepaid data.

AVIF is about 13 to 35% smaller and decodes in about 96% of browsers ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/avif)), but browsers cannot reliably encode it from a canvas. MDN lists only PNG as mandatory for `toBlob` and silently falls back to PNG for unsupported types ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/HTMLCanvasElement/toBlob)), and Firefox's request for AVIF encoding is still open ([Bugzilla](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1821907)). Keep WebP. After encoding, check that `blob.type === 'image/webp'`, and if the device returned a PNG, re-encode as JPEG at 0.8 so a large PNG never reaches the server.

Loading rules complete the picture. Every `<img>` needs `width`/`height` or `aspect-ratio`. Without dimensions the image defaults to 0×0, the layout shifts, and lazy loading can fetch the whole gallery at once. Chrome starts lazy-loading 2,500 px ahead of the viewport on connections slower than 4G, a value that is hardcoded. Combining `loading="lazy"` with `fetchpriority="high"` makes no sense ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/browser-level-image-lazy-loading)). Since 2,500 px is still about 15 to 25 cards, a catalogue of hundreds of items should be virtualised (TanStack Virtual or react-window) or at least given `content-visibility:auto`, which caps the number of decoded bitmaps.

## Recommended plan for Grocery Khata

**Step 1: grid and card (front end only, about 1 to 2 days).** Rebuild `ItemCard` to the 2-column spec above, with a reserved name height, pack size always visible, MRP and "% off" only when the discount is above zero, and a 48 px ADD/stepper target. Add the square fallback tile and `onError`. Give images `loading="lazy" decoding="async"` with width and height. Mark only the first 2 to 4 cards eager with `fetchpriority="high"`, because they are likely the largest contentful paint.

**Step 2: second rendition from the phone (about 1 day).** The phone already decodes and resizes the photo, so have it also encode a 320 px WebP and upload both images in one request. This needs no server image library. Lower the quality target to 0.8, step down to 0.7 and then 0.6 if a file is over budget, and set budgets of about **20 KB for the thumbnail and 60 KB for the full image**. Use `createImageBitmap` with `resizeWidth` where supported, so the browser never decodes the full 12 MP camera image, then shrink canvases to zero size and revoke object URLs after the upload. Store the thumbnail as a second bytea column or row, and expose it at the same public URL with a size parameter and the same `?v=` stamp and immutable headers. Render cards with `srcset="…thumb 320w, …full 512w" sizes="(max-width: 480px) 45vw, 180px"`.

**Step 3: backfill existing images.** For photos uploaded before Step 2, run a one-off job on the server. ImageSharp is free under the Six Labors Split License for companies with under $1M annual gross revenue ([NuGet licence](https://packages.nuget.org/packages/SixLabors.ImageSharp/3.1.3/License)), and NetVips benchmarks fastest ([Anthony Simmon](https://anthonysimmon.pages.dev/benchmarking-dotnet-libraries-for-image-resizing)). This also differs from our earlier research, which proposed server-side generation for every upload. Generating on the phone avoids CPU load on the server, and the server library is kept only for backfill.

**Step 4: caching and scale.** Add a cache-first Workbox runtime route for image URLs, with `maxEntries` of about 300 to 500 and `purgeOnQuotaError`. The versioned URLs never go stale. Postgres bytea is fine at this scale: 10,000 items at about 80 KB is roughly 800 MB. Putting Cloudflare's free plan in front of the existing URLs keeps repeat reads off the database. Move the bytes to R2 later if backups grow, at **$0.015/GB-month with zero egress fees** ([Cloudflare R2](https://cloudflare.com/en-ca/products/r2)).

**Step 5: image sources.** Ship "tap to add photo", with a square guide and auto-trim to white, as the default. Add an optional Open Food Facts lookup by EAN that copies the image into our own storage through the same resize pipeline and shows a "Photo: Open Food Facts (CC BY-SA)" credit on the detail view and on a credits page. Add a report-image/takedown flow and an IP policy page. Approach GS1 DataKart for pricing only once paid shops ask for ready-made pack shots. Never scrape marketplaces or Google Images.

## Conclusion

The visual upgrade and the memory fix turn out to be the same piece of work. A smaller card needs a smaller image, and the smaller image is what keeps low-end phones stable, so a single 320 px rendition pays off on both looks and RAM. The bigger strategic point is that image coverage is a catalogue problem, not a design problem. Licensed internet sources cover perhaps a fifth of packaged Indian food and almost none of the loose staples a kirana sells. Shop-taken photos shared by EAN under clear licence terms are therefore the only route that grows with the user base, and the fallback tile has to look good enough to be the normal state for months. Two items remain open before anyone copies exact measurements or relies on brand images: a screenshot audit of Blinkit and Flipkart cards at 360 px, and a lawyer's view on implied licence for brand pack shots.
